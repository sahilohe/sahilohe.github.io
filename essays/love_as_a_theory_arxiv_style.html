A Conjecture on Love

This essay presents a conjecture: love can involve a mind constructing an understanding of another person and becoming attached to it. The theory develops through experiences, memories, interpretations, and expectations. Because it becomes emotionally significant, the mind may resist revising it when reality challenges it. Moving on can then be understood as revising or abandoning a theory that no longer fits the evidence, not necessarily forgetting the person or erasing the emotions associated with them. The idea connects love to the problem of protecting cherished scientific theories from criticism and creates a self-referential test: a theory that explains resistance to criticism must itself remain open to criticism. This is a philosophical conjecture, not a claim that love is literally identical to scientific theorising.

Keywords: love, theory, attachment, criticism, moving on, critical rationalism, self-reference, propagation of ideas

1. Introduction

Love is often described as a feeling, a bond, or a special connection. Another way to examine it is to ask what a mind comes to believe about the person it loves. Through repeated interactions and remembered experiences, a person builds an explanation of who someone is, what their actions mean, and what might be expected from them.

The conjecture is that love involves more than feelings toward a person: it can also involve attachment to an understanding of that person. This understanding is constructed by the mind that loves. It is not the person themselves, and it is not guaranteed to represent them accurately.

2. Constructing a Theory of Someone

An understanding of another person is assembled from observations, memories, conversations, interpretations, and expectations. Some parts may be well supported by repeated experience; others may be assumptions or guesses. The mind uses this model to make sense of the person and to anticipate how they might act.

Because the theory is personal, two people can know the same individual and still hold different theories about them. Both theories may be incomplete, and either may be mistaken. The sincerity or intensity of love does not, by itself, establish the accuracy of the lover's understanding.

Core distinction: The person is not the same thing as the explanation a mind constructs about that person.

3. Attachment and Resistance to Revision

People can become attached to ideas as well as to people. An understanding of someone may become intertwined with memories, hopes, plans, and a person's understanding of their own past. Revising it can therefore be painful: it may require reinterpreting events or accepting that earlier expectations were mistaken.

When new behaviour conflicts with the theory, the mind has several options. It can revise its explanation, suspend judgement, seek more evidence, or reinterpret the new evidence to protect the old view. The last option can preserve the theory while preventing learning. For example, repeated distance might be explained away as proof that someone is secretly afraid of their own feelings. That explanation may be possible, but without evidence it can also function as a defence against criticism.

This is not a claim that everyone behaves this way, or that all persistence is irrational. A single contradiction may be misleading, and relationships require judgement across time and context. The concern is the possibility of refusing to revise an understanding regardless of what evidence appears.

4. Moving On as Revising a Theory

Under this conjecture, moving on can mean changing or abandoning the explanation one has built about someone when it no longer fits reality. The mind may stop expecting a relationship to become what it once imagined, stop interpreting every action through the old story, or accept that its understanding of the person was incomplete.

Moving on does not necessarily mean forgetting someone, denying that the relationship mattered, or instantly losing all affection. Memories and emotions may remain even after expectations have changed. The key change, in this interpretation, is epistemic: the mind no longer treats its earlier theory as something that must be preserved.

Reality does not always force revision automatically. People can resist criticism for a long time. Moving on is one possible result of encountering evidence and eventually allowing it to change one's understanding.

5. The Parallel with Scientific Theories

The proposed pattern resembles a familiar problem in science, although the resemblance alone does not establish a shared cause. A scientific theory is proposed to explain observations. When contrary evidence appears, a researcher can investigate it and revise the theory, or protect the theory by adding explanations that prevent it from being challenged. Attachment to a theory can make the second response tempting, but attachment is not a substitute for evidence.

Scientific theorisingA theory about someone loved
Constructed to explain observationsConstructed from experiences and interpretations
New results may conflict with predictionsSomeone's actions may conflict with expectations
A theory may be defended against criticismAn image of someone may be protected through reinterpretation
Criticism can lead to revision or rejectionCriticism of one's assumptions can lead to a changed understanding or moving on

The analogy has limits. Scientific inquiry ideally uses public evidence, explicit methods, and criticism by multiple people. A person's understanding of a loved one is often private, emotionally charged, and based on evidence that may be difficult to interpret. Love and science are not the same process. The proposed similarity, if useful, is narrower: in both cases, a cherished explanation can be protected from evidence that should prompt reconsideration.

6. The Propagation of Ideas About Love

Ideas about love can pass between minds through conversations, stories, films, literature, family practices, and culture. People inherit concepts of what love means, then use those concepts to interpret their own relationships. Their interpretations may, in turn, influence how they speak and act, giving the ideas further opportunities to spread.

This propagation does not mean that one person's private theory literally transfers unchanged into another mind. What spreads is a description, story, norm, or pattern of interpretation that another person reconstructs in their own mind. Each reconstruction can alter the idea.

7. The Strange Loop: Criticising the Theory of Love

The conjecture also turns back on itself. It proposes that minds can become attached to theories and resist criticism, but it is itself a theory constructed by a mind. Its author can become attached to it, defend it against counterexamples, and reinterpret evidence to preserve it.

If that happens, the theory performs the very behaviour it describes. This would be a self-referential loop, reminiscent of the idea of a “strange loop”: a system's explanation turns back toward the system producing or holding that explanation. It is not automatically a logical paradox. The conjecture can still be accurate, mistaken, incomplete, or in need of refinement.

A critical approach therefore asks what would count against the conjecture. One possible answer is a better explanation of love, one that accounts for the same observations and also explains phenomena this account cannot. If such an explanation succeeds more fully, the present theory should be revised or replaced.

8. Criticisms and Open Questions

This conjecture explains only part of what people call love: how a mind forms and preserves an understanding of another person, and how that understanding may resist criticism. It does not yet explain love as a whole.

Several central questions remain open:

  • Why does a mind construct a theory about one person rather than another?
  • What distinguishes a theory of someone loved from a theory of someone merely known?
  • What makes that theory emotionally significant?
  • Why can love persist through separation or rejection?
  • Why do some people move on while others remain attached for years?
  • Why can love motivate care, sacrifice, protection, or destructive behaviour?

A further problem is whether the parallel with scientific theorising is more than a structural analogy. Scientists and people in love can both resist revising cherished explanations, but this resemblance does not prove that the same underlying mechanisms cause both forms of resistance.

The conjecture would explain too little if it treated every persistent attachment as resistance to criticism. Some people remain attached for reasons other than mistaken beliefs, and not every contradiction should force a theory to be abandoned. Evidence can be incomplete, behaviour can change, and relationships require interpretation across time and context.

These criticisms leave the conjecture open. Its next step is to identify a specific phenomenon it explains better than existing accounts, clarify the mechanisms it proposes, and consider what observations would count against it. A better explanation of love should replace this one if it accounts for the evidence more successfully.

9. Conclusion

One possible aspect of love is that a mind builds an understanding of another person and may become attached to it. When reality challenges that theory, the mind can revise it, defend it, or refuse to let it go. Moving on can involve changing or abandoning the theory, not necessarily erasing the person from memory. This account explains only part of the phenomenon and leaves the origins and mechanisms of love unresolved.

If the conjecture is useful, it may connect one aspect of love to a broader problem: cherished explanations can resist criticism, and ideas about love can spread from mind to mind. The conjecture must also face its own claim. It cannot ask people to question their explanations while exempting itself from question.

The sincerity of love does not guarantee the accuracy of one's understanding. This conjecture remains tentative and should be revised or rejected if a better explanation accounts for the evidence more successfully.

References and Intellectual Context

This essay is a conceptual exploration developed from the conversation, not a literature review. Its discussion of criticism and revisability is broadly related to critical rationalism, especially the work of Karl Popper. Its use of “strange loop” is an analogy to self-reference explored by Douglas Hofstadter. These connections are contextual, not claims that either thinker proposed this theory of love.
